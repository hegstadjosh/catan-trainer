Development cards

Costs 1 sheep + 1 wheat + 1 ore. Compare it with the best thing those cards could build instead, not with doing nothing.

Should you buy one now?

Wheat + ore also go toward a city. A city is a certain +1 VP and more income; a dev card is a random, usually delayed ability. Weigh each possible outcome by its chance, then compare that value with your best available build.

You are at 9 VP
A fresh-deck buy has a 20% chance of a VP card and an immediate win. Most other draws cannot help this turn.
You have played 2 knights
A third played knight can claim Largest Army (+2 VP) if nobody already holds it. To take the award from someone, you must exceed their played knights. A new purchase must wait until a later turn.
The robber is on your best hex
A knight restores that production and steals one card if the victim has any. It can also block an opponent.
You need roads
Road Building replaces up to 2 wood + 2 brick. Two roads you do not need are not “4 useful cards.”
You are missing one pair
Year of Plenty gives 2 chosen cards but costs 3 to buy. Its value is choice and timing, not card profit.
Opponents are piling a resource
Monopoly’s return depends on their hands. There is no fixed expected payout without a model of those hands.
Formal decision rule
Q(s,a) = E[V(next state) | s, a]
Buy a dev card iff Q(s, dev) > max Q(s, alternative)

Use one objective for every action: ideally the probability of winning, or a stated approximation. For a simple utility model, E[U(dev)] = Σ p(card)·U(card | position) − (λsheep + λwheat + λore). Each λ is that resource’s current marginal value, not automatically 1. Never add VP and resource counts without stating their conversion.

Defaults are a fresh standard deck (25 cards). Edited counts are a scenario, not a tracker: cards opponents hold unrevealed are still unknown. You cannot play a non-VP card on the turn you buy it, and you may play at most one per turn. VP cards count immediately.

Chance of drawing what you need

Counts cards drawn, not knights played or an award secured. Assumes a uniform shuffle and no other player buying.

How this is computed
P(X ≥ q) = Σj≥q C(K,j)·C(N−K, b−j) / C(N,b)
E[buys to q successes] = q(N+1)/(K+1)

N = cards in the assumed deck; K = target cards; b = purchases; q = copies needed. Draws are without replacement (hypergeometric). The expectation is finite only when K ≥ q.

Knight: production restored

Assumes you already hold a playable knight and the robber would otherwise stay for that many rolls; another robber move may cut this short. Add the theft only if the victim has cards. Army progress and harm to opponents are separate.

How this is computed
Expected cards restored = pips × units × rolls / 36

Pips are the number of dice combinations (out of 36) that roll the hex’s number: 5 for a 6 or 8, down to 1 for a 2 or 12.

Common misreadings

  • Minimum ≠ expectation. Three fresh-deck buys are all knights only 15.8% of the time. The mean number of buys to a third knight is 5.2.
  • “20% VP” ≠ “0.2 VP of value.” That counts direct VP only; it omits army progress, production, theft and progress cards.
  • Buying power ≠ playing speed. Even holding three knights, playing all three takes three of your turns. New purchases add a one-turn wait.
  • Draws are not independent. Each draw changes the deck. Use hypergeometric odds for a fixed deck, not repeated 56% coin flips.
